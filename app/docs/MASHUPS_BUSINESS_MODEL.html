01

Bottom line

Mashups should not become another royalty-free music library or another open remix community.

It should become a managed marketplace and campaign operating system for commercially usable short-form audio.

Creators buy speed, confidence, measurable performance, and claims support.

Artists provide one-stop music and receive licensing income, usage bonuses, exposure, and performance data.

Brands fund campaigns, challenges, creators, and custom tracks.

The strongest business model is:

  1. Creator and studio subscriptions for workflow.
  2. Scoped commercial licenses for higher-risk uses.
  3. Sponsored challenges and managed campaigns for meaningful revenue.
  4. A curated, directly contracted music catalog.
  5. Artist payouts combining usage pools, premium license revenue, and selective upfront commissions.

Legal scope: This should be treated as a US-first strategy and reviewed by a music-licensing attorney before live agreements are issued.

02

Why the opportunity exists

Creators currently choose between familiar music they may not be allowed to use and safe music that often does not perform.

  • Use popular music and hope platform licenses cover them.
  • Use generic stock music that does not perform.
  • Buy a license that only works on one platform or one video.
  • Use platform music that cannot be remixed, exported, sponsored, or reused.
  • Receive a copyright claim despite having purchased a license.
  • Spend hours searching for music rather than creating content.

The platform gap

YouTube Creator Music licenses generally apply to one long-form YouTube video, cannot transfer to other platforms, do not cover Shorts, prohibit remixing, and restrict brand-promotional content. YouTube usage details, YouTube restrictions

TikTok says commercial users should use its Commercial Music Library because licenses outside that library generally do not cover commercial posts. Its library is large, but the permission is fundamentally TikTok-centric. TikTok music guidance, TikTok Commercial Music Library

Instagram says its normal licensed music library is intended for personal, non-commercial use. Instagram music guidance

One track. Multiple platforms. Permitted edits. Commercial clarity. Campaign attribution. A human claims desk.

That is substantially more valuable than "50,000 royalty-free tracks."

04

Customer segments

1. Monetized creators

These creators make money from audiences, memberships, subscriptions, affiliate links, products, or platform monetization. Their music use may be commercial even when they are not being paid by a third-party brand. Mashups should call this commercial self-promotion, not ordinary personal use.

Examples include fitness, beauty, fashion, gaming, nightlife, coaching, education, OnlyFans and subscription creators, and musicians promoting releases.

2. Sponsored creators

These creators are being paid to promote someone else's product or service. They need sponsor-safe licenses, proof that can be sent to a brand, paid amplification options, claims response, and usage tied to a specific campaign. This use should cost more than ordinary creator content.

3. Editors, managers, and agencies

They need client-specific licenses, multiple seats, transferable project coverage, campaign templates, claims records, API access, central billing, and indemnity at higher tiers. This is likely to become the healthiest recurring SaaS segment.

4. Brands

Brands should not merely subscribe to the catalog. They should buy productized campaigns that combine a custom or curated track, creator cohort, campaign brief, licensed edits, challenge, attribution links, performance dashboard, paid-media rights, and claims operations.

5. Producers and artists

The supplier promise should be simple:

  • Keep ownership of your music.
  • Keep your PRO and ordinary streaming income where contracts permit.
  • Receive premium license revenue.
  • Participate in a transparent usage pool.
  • Receive performance data you cannot get from a conventional stock library.
  • Get your music used by creators before or alongside DSP release.
  • No AI training without a separate opt-in agreement.

08

How to acquire tracks

Build supply through four distinct lanes.

1. Commissioned originals

Commission producers against specific creator-content briefs: fitness transformation, beauty reveal, nightlife transition, gaming montage, luxury lifestyle, comedy misdirection, product unboxing, sports highlight, fashion walk, or mature-audience self-promotion.

Do not brief producers with vague genres such as "make a trap song." A better brief is:

Produce a 75-second track with a clean 15-second escalation, an unmistakable transition at second 8, no lyrical conflict, and stems suitable for fitness transformation videos.

Initial commission economics could be:

  • $500-$1,500 per accepted 60-90 second master
  • Non-recoupable fee
  • Artist retains ownership
  • Mashups receives broad sync, editing, and sublicensing rights
  • Mashups receives a 6-12 month exclusive UGC administration window
  • Artist continues participating in license and usage payouts

A mature competitor such as Epidemic Sound reports paying $2,000-$8,000 per commissioned track, plus streaming splits and a usage bonus. Mashups cannot initially compete on upfront fees, so it must compete on ownership retention, flexibility, transparent upside, and direct performance data. Epidemic Sound artist model

2. Existing one-stop catalogs

Recruit independent producers who control both the master and composition. The ideal catalog partner has 20-100 original tracks, no uncleared samples, few co-writers, control over publishing, clear distributor and Content ID status, stems, interest in creator distribution, and music that is good but under-monetized.

This lane can have little or no upfront payment, but a more generous revenue split.

3. Sponsored commissions

Brands fund custom tracks or packs. Mashups avoids financing all supply, producers receive better upfront compensation, brands receive campaign-specific sound, and tracks can later enter the broader catalog if contracts permit.

4. Curated open submissions

Open submissions can become an acquisition funnel, but should not automatically populate the catalog. Use monthly briefs, referral-only submissions initially, a clear acceptance rate, rights documentation before audio review, and payments only for accepted inventory.

No "upload anything and declare you own it" public licensing.

10

Artist compensation

A strong artist deal combines predictable and performance-based compensation.

Commissioned tracks

  • Upfront non-recoupable fee
  • 50-60% of premium license net revenue
  • Share of subscription usage pool
  • Artist retains DSP and applicable PRO royalties
  • Artist retains ownership

Existing catalog tracks

  • Little or no upfront fee
  • 65-70% of premium license net revenue
  • Share of subscription usage pool
  • Artist retains ownership and outside exploitation rights

Sponsored custom tracks

  • Larger upfront production fee
  • Campaign sync payment
  • Bonus for performance or expanded usage
  • Negotiated rights for broader catalog reuse

Subscription usage pool

Allocate approximately 15-25% of net music-bearing subscription revenue into an artist pool. Do not distribute it based on downloads. Downloads are easy to manipulate and do not demonstrate actual value.

WeightSignal
50%Unique issued campaign licenses
30%Verified published posts
20%Normalized performance

Normalize performance against the creator's historical baseline. Use a logarithmic cap on views so that 100,000 views is more valuable than 10,000 but not automatically worth ten times as much. Publish the formula. Opaque royalty formulas destroy supplier trust.

11

The rights reality

A song contains at least two distinct copyright interests: the musical composition and the sound recording.

They may have different owners. The U.S. Copyright Office explicitly treats them as separate works. U.S. Copyright Office

For video use, Mashups needs synchronization authority for the composition and master-use authority for the recording. The MLC's blanket license is audio-only and does not cover audiovisual synchronization. The MLC licensing scope

Performing-rights organizations are not a substitute for sync clearance. SESAC states that sync rights generally require all publishers, while use of an existing recording also requires the recording owner. SESAC licensing FAQ

Remixing creates another problem. The Copyright Office states that the copyright owner controls derivative versions, and changing someone else's work does not make it yours without permission. Copyright Office fair-use FAQ

A checkbox saying "I own this" is not a rights system.

13

Supplier contract requirements

The agreement should let Mashups

  • Host and stream previews.
  • Reproduce and deliver licensed files.
  • Sublicense tracks to creators, agencies, and brands.
  • Synchronize tracks with audiovisual content.
  • Cut, loop, fade, normalize, time-shift, and create platform-length versions.
  • Provide instrumental, clean, and stem versions.
  • Permit defined remix and fork behavior.
  • Use track and artist metadata for promotion.
  • Issue immutable end-user license certificates.
  • Resolve claims and communicate with distributors.
  • Keep already-published licensed projects valid after the supply term ends.

The supplier should warrant

  • Control of 100% of the master.
  • Control or valid authority over 100% of the composition.
  • Approval from every co-writer, publisher, performer, producer, and featured artist.
  • No undisclosed samples or interpolations.
  • No conflicting beat lease.
  • No conflicting sync representation.
  • Accurate PRO, publisher, ISRC, ISWC, and distributor information.
  • No unauthorized voice or likeness replicas.
  • Disclosure of materially AI-generated elements.
  • No conflicting Content ID registration or claim policy.

The contract should include an explicit sample manifest, even when the answer is "no samples."

License product design

Licenses should be project-specific and immutable.

Each issued license should record:

  • License ID
  • Exact track and version
  • Master owner
  • Composition owner or administrator
  • Creator or company
  • Registered social channels
  • Platforms
  • Project type
  • Sponsor or client, when applicable
  • Organic, sponsored, or paid-media status
  • Paid-media spend cap
  • Territory
  • Start date
  • Publication deadline
  • Whether existing posts survive cancellation
  • Permitted edits
  • Prohibited contexts
  • Attribution requirement
  • Claim contact
  • Immutable terms snapshot
  • Hash of the licensed audio file

The public verification page should expose enough to prove validity without exposing addresses, private contracts, or payment records.

Important survival rule

Projects lawfully created and published while a subscription or project license is active should generally remain licensed after cancellation. The customer should lose the ability to create new projects, not wake up to claims on old videos.

Both Artlist and Soundstripe describe versions of this active-subscription/project-survival approach, so customers increasingly expect it. Artlist license explanation, Soundstripe licensing guide

17

Sensitive-use permissions

The product should support track-level opt-ins for mature or adult-adjacent content, alcohol, gambling, cannabis, politics, religion, weapons, cryptocurrency, medical products, dating, and advocacy.

This matters particularly if OnlyFans creators remain an acquisition wedge. YouTube Creator Music explicitly restricts adult content and brand-focused promotional use, which creates opportunity, but artists must knowingly opt into those contexts. YouTube restrictions

Mashups must distinguish lawful mature-audience creator promotion, explicit audiovisual content, content prohibited by the destination platform, and content an artist does not want associated with their identity.

A music license does not override TikTok, Instagram, or YouTube content policies.
18

Content ID strategy

Content ID is both a defense mechanism and a major source of accidental claims.

YouTube requires exclusive rights for Content ID references and says mashups, remixes, and non-exclusively licensed material commonly do not qualify. YouTube Content ID eligibility

Therefore Mashups should not indiscriminately register everything in Content ID.

Mashups Managed

Mashups or its rights-administration partner receives exclusive UGC administration authority for specific tracks and territories. Mashups manages allowlisting, claims, and release policies.

Partner Managed

The original artist, label, or distributor keeps Content ID administration but signs a claims SLA and provides allowlisting or rapid release support.

Claim canary

  1. Generate private test videos using each planned hook cut.
  2. Upload them to test channels.
  3. Wait for automated claims.
  4. Identify distributors, publishers, and third parties claiming the track.
  5. Resolve conflicts before issuing green licenses.
  6. Re-run tests after distributor or ownership changes.

Do not register creator-made forks or mashups as Content ID references unless exclusive rights in every element are proven.

19

Claims operations

Do not promise "claim-free." Even correctly licensed tracks can trigger automated systems.

Promise clear license evidence, fast response, claim release support, transparent status, escalation, and a replacement track when necessary.

TierFirst responseTarget resolution
Free2 business daysBest effort
Creator24 hours72 hours
Creator Pro12 hours48 hours
Studio4 business hours24 hours
EnterpriseContractedContracted

Every dispute package should contain the license certificate, audio hash, ISRC, ownership record, permitted-use summary, channel and project ID, publication date, supplier contact, Content ID administrator, and supporting contract reference.

Quarantine a track automatically when multiple unrelated creators receive claims, ownership becomes disputed, contributor approval fails, sample conflicts appear, a distributor refuses allowlisting, or rights metadata no longer reconciles.

Hold a small royalty reserve for chargebacks, reversals, and unresolved supplier claims.

20

Indemnity

Indemnity can be valuable, but it should be sold carefully.

Soundstripe currently advertises business and enterprise coverage including indemnification, with enterprise coverage up to $1 million. That is a useful market anchor, but it requires contracts, operational controls, and insurance. Soundstripe pricing and licensing

  1. No broad indemnity during closed beta.
  2. Limited contractual warranty for properly licensed uses.
  3. Liability capped to a multiple of fees on creator plans.
  4. Higher negotiated caps for agencies.
  5. Media liability or errors-and-omissions insurance before major brand work.
  6. Insurance-backed enterprise indemnity only after claims history is known.
Never offer unlimited indemnity.
21

AI music policy

Do not use AI-generated music as cheap catalog filler.

The Copyright Office says AI-assisted work can be protected, but purely AI-generated material or material without sufficient human control may not be. Prompts alone generally do not establish authorship. Copyright Office AI report

  • Human-made and human-controlled catalog by default.
  • Mandatory disclosure of AI tools.
  • No cloned voices or identifiable artist imitation.
  • No unlicensed training representations.
  • No AI training on supplier tracks without separate opt-in and compensation.
  • Store tool name, model, date, source inputs, and human contribution description.
  • Give brands and creators an "AI-free catalog" filter.
  • Keep AI-adapted versions in a separate rights and compensation program.

Artist trust can become a differentiator.

Distribution strategy

Mashups should not become a record distributor immediately.

  1. Acquire direct campaign and UGC rights.
  2. Let artists retain ordinary DSP distribution.
  3. Require disclosure of distributor and Content ID administrator.
  4. Establish allowlisting procedures.
  5. Consider one distribution partner after the catalog proves demand.
  6. Offer optional release services later.

Launch pattern

  • DAY -21Track delivered and rights reviewed
  • DAY -14Private creator testing
  • DAY -7Hook cuts and claims testing
  • DAY 0Weekly Pack release
  • DAY +7Winning cut gets challenge support
  • DAY +14Expanded creator seeding

This gives creators early access while helping artists build streaming demand.

A&R should be data-driven

Do not rank music by total views. Large creators would dominate.

The most useful track score is not "plays." It is closer to:

Expected creator lift
x export-to-post probability
x rights reliability
x audience fit

Use creator-normalized performance:

Post performance / creator's trailing baseline

This is also how the Momentum Feed and Creator Scoreboard should work.

The flywheel

  1. Mashups commissions or acquires a one-stop track.
  2. The track becomes three campaign-ready hooks.
  3. Creators receive a clear license and campaign package.
  4. Creators publish with attribution.
  5. Mashups measures normalized performance.
  6. Winning tracks rise in the Momentum Feed.
  7. Producers earn more and submit better music.
  8. Brands fund challenges around proven tracks.
  9. Creator results improve.
  10. More creators and producers join.
The moat is not the audio file.

The moat is the combined rights graph, claims history, hook-performance data, creator-track matching model, attribution graph, producer relationship network, and brand campaign results.

Go-to-market

Creator wedge

Start with high-frequency creators who already spend money to acquire audiences. A mixed founding cohort is safer than making the company synonymous with one vertical:

  • 15 fitness creators
  • 10 beauty or fashion creators
  • 10 gaming creators
  • 5 nightlife creators
  • 5 mature-audience subscription creators
  • 5 editors or managers

OnlyFans and mature creators can be a valuable vertical because their posts are commercially motivated and mainstream libraries may have restrictions. Use a separately opted-in catalog rather than forcing every artist into that association.

Producer wedge

Recruit the Founding 25 through direct outreach to independent sync producers, music schools, producer Discord communities, small labels, beatmakers with one-stop catalogs, creator-producers, referrals, and monthly paid briefs.

Keep your masters. Keep your streaming income. Earn when creators license and publish your music. See which hooks actually perform. No AI training without your permission.

Agency wedge

Recruit five small creator-management or editing agencies. One agency with 20 active creators is operationally more valuable than 20 unrelated consumer accounts because it creates lower acquisition cost, higher retention, more predictable volume, better campaign data, easier rights education, and higher willingness to pay.

26

Referral economics

The recurring referral concept should be bounded.

  • 20% of net subscription revenue
  • Maximum 12 paid months
  • 60-day chargeback hold
  • No self-referrals
  • No referral payout on taxes, refunds, or premium music pass-through
  • Agency partners receive negotiated volume discounts instead
  • Producer referrals can receive one-time accepted-track bounties

An uncapped lifetime recurring percentage can become a permanent margin problem and attract fraud.

27

Trust and legal operations

Because Mashups stores user-uploaded material, it should implement formal DMCA operations.

The Copyright Office says relevant online service providers seeking safe-harbor protection must register a designated agent, publish that contact information, respond expeditiously, and maintain required policies. Copyright Office DMCA directory, Section 512 guidance

  • Register a DMCA agent.
  • Publish a valid takedown address.
  • Maintain counter-notice procedures.
  • Implement a repeat-infringer policy.
  • Record every moderation action.
  • Keep rights and enforcement teams separate from ordinary support.
  • Establish emergency track quarantine.
  • Retain immutable notice and response records.

The existing schema has useful beginnings, but contracts and operating procedures are more important than database tables.

Failure modes

Creators only want famous music

Sell outcomes, not catalog size. Use emerging artists with distinctive hooks, prove performance with normalized data, offer custom creator sounds, and pursue recognizable label catalogs only after demand is proven.

Tracks trigger claims

Use a one-stop catalog, claim-canary testing, known Content ID administrators, automatic quarantine, strong evidence packages, and a claims SLA.

Producers do not trust the platform

Let artists retain ownership, publish the payout formula, pay non-recoupable commissions, prohibit AI training without opt-in, provide monthly statements and audit rights, and keep existing projects valid after catalog removal.

Subscription margins collapse

Limit included legal entitlements, keep paid ads separate, allocate a fixed royalty pool, use project credits rather than unlimited commercial rights, push studios and agencies, and fund custom supply through brands.

Rights operations become an agency

Standardize license SKUs and campaign packages. Do not provide bespoke legal review on creator plans. Enterprise customers pay for custom terms. Automate evidence collection, not legal judgment.

The Mashups brand suggests infringement

Keep the company name, but reframe it around creators, campaigns, and sounds. Use "licensed edits," "forks," and "original flips." Never market unauthorized combinations as safe. Place classic copyrighted-song mashups in an unmonetized experimental lane or remove them.

32

Decisions to make now

  1. Position Mashups as a campaign-safe audio network, not a stock library.
  2. Launch with only directly contracted one-stop tracks.
  3. Let artists retain ownership.
  4. Pay artists through upfront commissions, premium splits, and a transparent usage pool.
  5. Keep ordinary organic rights in subscriptions.
  6. Charge separately for sponsorships, paid media, client work, and standalone releases.
  7. Make studios, agencies, and brands the economic engine.
  8. Use mature-audience licensing only through explicit artist opt-in.
  9. Promise fast claim resolution, not impossible "claim-free" guarantees.
  10. Start with 100 masters and 300 hooks, not thousands of tracks.
  11. Treat rights completeness and performance data as the moat.
  12. Do not scale paid creator acquisition until claims and publishing conversion meet hard gates.

Mashups finds or commissions music designed to perform in short-form content, contracts every relevant right, packages the best hooks into ready-to-publish campaigns, protects creators when claims occur, measures the resulting lift, and shares the revenue with the people who made the music.

Act II / Recognizable music

Real music is not the pipe dream. Asking for every right to every hit is.

33

Real music verdict

Commercially released, recognizable music is accessible. The winning strategy is to narrow the permission, not lower the ambition.

Mashups should not try to purchase an unlimited blanket catalog of current hits. It should become the rights and campaign infrastructure connecting artists who need distribution, creators who need effective music, and brands that need measurable cultural reach.

The music does not always need to be purchased. Artists and labels can grant the rights and fund the campaign because Mashups is helping break their records.

What is achievable

  • Human-made original music from credible independent artists.
  • Recognizable emerging artists with real listeners and social momentum.
  • Selected established and back-catalog tracks through a commercial licensing partner.
  • Major-label clips inside a tightly controlled app and sharing workflow.
  • Individually cleared mainstream songs for specific brand campaigns.
  • Famous music used through platform-native sound workflows without Mashups distributing the audio.

What is unrealistic initially

Every popular song, every territory, every platform, unlimited remixing, permanent downloadable exports, creator monetization, paid advertising, broad sublicensing, and no minimum guarantee under one inexpensive blanket license.

Operating principle: Sell narrow, auditable permission units. Do not ask rightsholders for every conceivable use before Mashups has demonstrated demand.

Five access rails

The catalog should be a routing system. Each track enters through the rail that matches its rights and economics.

1. Mashups Open

Build a direct catalog where one party controls the recording and composition. These artists grant permission to cut approved portions, export music inside videos, post organically across approved platforms, monetize eligible posts, create approved loops and transitions, keep published posts live after campaign expiration, participate in challenges, and use approved stems.

This is the highest-margin catalog and should power Weekly Viral Packs, the Hook Generator, Challenge Engine, templates, and Remix Forks.

2. Release Launch

Labels, managers, distributors, and artists provide selected new releases under a short campaign schedule. Mashups provides the creator cohort, campaign workflow, incentives, publishing verification, and performance reporting. The rightsholder can become a paying customer rather than a royalty recipient.

3. Embedded Commercial Clips

Use a partner such as Feed Clips to surface pre-cleared commercial clips within Mashups. This could provide major-label familiarity without Mashups negotiating every rightsholder separately, but the integration must enforce the partner's territories, platforms, reporting, fingerprinting, withdrawal, and editing rules.

4. Premium Campaign Checkout

Offer a visible upgrade for individually licensed recognizable music. Possible implementations include an embedded partner integration, referral checkout, agency-assisted clearance, Mashups purchasing a scoped campaign license, or passing the license through as a brand campaign line item.

Lickd's public product shape fits high-value social campaigns better than an unlimited creator subscription: defined videos, platforms, duration, territory, term, and paid-boost scope.

5. Platform-Native Handoff

Mashups can help creators work with songs available in a destination platform without distributing those audio files.

  1. The creator selects a song for timing and creative planning.
  2. Mashups generates the beat map, captions, edit points, and visual cut plan.
  3. Mashups exports the video without copyrighted audio.
  4. The creator opens the destination platform.
  5. The creator adds the official sound from that platform's library.
  6. Mashups records the resulting post URL for attribution.

Important: Platform-native handoff creates no new rights. It cannot promise commercial use, paid advertising, cross-platform reuse, or continuing availability. It must be labeled NATIVE, never OPEN.

Additional music supply

The best catalog may come from music the traditional sync market is not actively activating.

  1. Dormant label catalogs.

Partner reality

Marketing pages prove the category exists. Only the signed agreement determines whether a partner actually covers the Mashups product.

Feed Clips opportunity

The public Feed Clips implementation guide says applications can offer curated or user-selected clips up to 60 seconds, synchronize them with UGC, use signed URLs, and report plays, additions, shares, and downloads.

That guide also contains material limits:

  • Its current implementation section says US territories only, while another Feed product page uses worldwide language.
  • Third-party sharing destinations must be contractually approved.
  • Downloads are described as personal use.
  • Derivative alteration is prohibited without permission.
  • Looping requires prior approval.
  • Tracks can lose rights and existing in-app synced media may need to be blocked.
  • User-upload applications must integrate ACRCloud fingerprinting.
  • Every play, track audition, addition, share, and download must be reported.

Lickd opportunity

Lickd's public brand offering describes a basic Social Sync license for up to five short-form videos, up to 60 seconds of music per video, 12 months, worldwide territory, and one month of paid boosting, with larger creator-led packages available. Blanket-license reality

A famous song is normally at least two legal assets, often controlled by multiple parties, with different policies and commercial expectations.

The sound recording and the underlying musical composition are separate copyrighted works. Permission for one is not a substitute for permission for the other. US Copyright Office: compositions and recordings

The Mechanical Licensing Collective administers eligible US streaming and download mechanical licenses, not synchronization, performance, or sound-recording licenses. The MLC license scope

A broad Mashups grant could require

  • Master synchronization rights.
  • Composition synchronization rights from every required share.
  • Permission to sublicense creators.
  • Downloadable video exports.
  • Distribution to named social platforms.
  • Creator monetization.
  • Commercial self-promotion and brand sponsorship.
  • Paid-media use and whitelisting.
  • Derivative edits, clips, loops, transitions, pitch, tempo, and stems.
  • Multiple territories and languages.
  • Content ID and claims operations.
  • Usage reporting, royalty accounting, and audits.
  • Catalog withdrawal and survival rules.
  • Brand-safety, sensitive-content, and artist-approval controls.
  • Minimum guarantees, advances, per-use royalties, or revenue shares.

That bundle is expensive because it shifts substantial risk and control away from the rightsholder. Mashups should unbundle it into auditable campaign, clip, project, platform, territory, and paid-media units.

False shortcuts

  • A Spotify, Apple Music, or YouTube consumer API does not provide sync or export rights.
  • Buying a download does not buy a commercial synchronization license.
  • ASCAP, BMI, SESAC, or MLC coverage is not a blanket sync solution.
  • A compulsory cover license does not provide video synchronization rights.
  • Fair use is not a dependable product policy.
  • A distributor agreement is insufficient when the distributor lacks composition-sync authority.
  • Content ID monetization or claim release is not retroactive permission.
  • Scraped stems and unofficial downloads are not legitimate supply.

Deal readiness

Rightsholders will evaluate operational credibility before they evaluate the interface.

  1. Every use maps to a user, track, license, export, destination, and post record.
  2. Unlicensed uploads can be fingerprinted, blocked, quarantined, or stripped.
  3. Territory, platform, term, and account eligibility can be enforced.
  4. Expired or withdrawn tracks are disabled for new creation immediately.
  5. Content ID claims can be allowlisted, reconciled, and audited.
  6. Rightsholders receive accurate, timely usage reports and payments.
  7. Sensitive industries and content categories can be restricted by track.
  8. Artists and rightsholders have clear opt-out and emergency-quarantine controls.
  9. Music, stems, and derived data are not used for AI training without a separate opt-in.
  10. Takedown requests can be executed quickly across the Mashups product.
  11. Creator identity, channels, campaign eligibility, and payouts are verifiable.
  12. Mashups maintains appropriate media liability, cyber, and errors-and-omissions coverage.
  13. Royalty statements, campaign payments, rights changes, and enforcement actions are auditable.
These controls are not back-office overhead. They are the product that earns access to progressively better music.
